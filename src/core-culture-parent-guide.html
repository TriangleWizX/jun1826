Core Culture Parent Guide

Help your child become calm, capable, and safe on the mat. Jiu-jitsu gives your child a place to solve hard problems with a partner.

Your child does not need every hard moment removed. They need hard moments small enough to work through safely.

How we develop capability on the mat

Your child learns to move, think, communicate, recover from mistakes, and keep working under resistance.

We track progress through:

  • Attendance
  • Safe training
  • Focus and effort
  • Better decisions
  • Respect for partners
  • Independence
  • Skill under resistance

Your child will develop at their own pace. We coach from their current skill level and help them take the next step.

Our roles on and off the mat

Coaches

  • Match your child with suitable partners
  • Adjust each activity for age, size, experience, and confidence
  • Give beginners a clear starting point
  • Use live resistance in controlled situations
  • Teach your child to protect themselves and their partner
  • Increase the challenge as your child improves
  • Give specific feedback
  • Contact you when attendance, behavior, or safety needs attention

Parents

  • Encourage from the side & let coaches instruct
  • Ask questions that highlight effort and decisions
  • Support class rules and behavior expectations
  • Give your child age-appropriate responsibility
  • Measure progress against their past work, not others
  • Let your child work through productive struggle
  • Keep the ride home supportive and stress-free

Students

  • Arrive ready to train
  • Listen when instruction starts
  • Stay with the assigned activity
  • Use safe resistance
  • Respect coaches and teammates
  • Try before asking to stop
  • Take care of their equipment
  • Finish each round with effort
Our shared responsibility Coaches plan and lead the class. The academy and family design a workable routine together; parents provide encouragement and useful information. Students practice effort, responsibility, and partner safety. Your child learns more than jiu-jitsu when all three groups handle their role. They learn to face pressure, make decisions, and keep control of their body.

Your role as a parent

What to reinforce

You do not need to coach jiu-jitsu from the side. Keep it simple: listen to the coach, keep working through an appropriate challenge, and take care of your partner. Tapping, pain, fear, or an unsafe situation always means stop and communicate—not “push through.”

1. Let the coaches give instruction

Encourage your child from the side.

You can say:

  • “Keep working.”
  • “Take your time.”
  • “Try another idea.”
  • “Good effort.”
  • “Listen to your coach.”

Please do not call out moves, scores, or corrections during class.

Your child needs to hear one set of instructions. They also need room to make decisions without waiting for an adult to solve each problem.

2. Ask about the class

Skip “Did you win?”

Ask:

  • What game did you play?
  • What felt hard?
  • What did you figure out?
  • What did your partner do well?
  • How did you help your partner?
  • What made you proud?
  • What do you want to try next class?
  • What part did you enjoy?

These questions help your child notice effort, decisions, and improvement.

3. Support the class rules

We ask each student to:

  • Arrive ready to train
  • Listen when instruction starts
  • Stay with the assigned activity
  • Use safe resistance
  • Respect coaches and teammates
  • Try before asking to stop
  • Take care of their equipment
  • Finish each round with effort

Your child will need reminders. We expect them to accept the reminder, make the correction, and return to the activity.

4. Give your child responsibility

Ask your child to handle tasks that match their age. They can learn to:

  • Pack their training clothes
  • Carry their bag
  • Bring water
  • Track their belongings
  • Put shoes in the correct place
  • Know their class days
  • Help clean the training area
  • Tell a coach when they need help

You may need to help a younger child. Reduce that help as they gain confidence.

5. Measure your child against their past work

One child may learn a movement fast. Another may show more patience, courage, focus, or control.

Avoid comparing:

  • Belts
  • Stripes
  • Competition results
  • Athletic ability
  • Class placement
  • Learning speed

Look at the child who first entered the room. Notice what your child can handle now.

6. Let your child struggle with the problem

Your child needs time to:

  1. Try an idea
  2. Meet resistance
  3. Make a mistake
  4. Adjust
  5. Try again
  6. Manage frustration
  7. Ask for help
  8. Stay calm

Do not step in when your child can still work through the problem.

A coach will step in when your child becomes unsafe, overwhelmed, or unable to continue the activity.

Hard rounds teach your child how to think and act under pressure. The goal is not a child who always knows the answer; it is a child who can stay engaged long enough to find one.

7. Keep the ride home supportive

Your child knows when a round felt hard.

You can say:

  • “I liked watching you work.”
  • “You kept trying.”
  • “I saw you help your partner.”
  • “You looked more comfortable today.”
  • “What part did you enjoy?”

Do not replay mistakes or give technical corrections unless your child asks for help.

Let the coaches handle technique. Give your child support and perspective.

Signs of progress

You may notice your child:

  • Enter class without hesitation
  • Train with a new partner
  • Stay calm in a difficult position
  • Ask a clear question
  • Use less force
  • Remember a class rule
  • Recover after frustration
  • Help a newer student
  • Try again after losing a round
  • Make a better decision than the week before

Belts and stripes mark progress. Observed capability and practice inform it. Attendance creates more opportunities for Sandy to observe, coach, and connect with your child; it is not a Report Card capability state. Track your child's current strengths and coaching priorities with our Progress Tracking Report Card.

Logistics and Class Preparation

Before class

Please arrive about 10 minutes early.

Your child should bring:

  • A clean gi or clean no-gi clothes
  • A labeled water bottle
  • Sandals or shoes for walking off the mat
  • A bag that holds all belongings
  • Any required protective equipment

Before class:

  • Trim fingernails and toenails
  • Remove jewelry
  • Secure long hair
  • Cover any approved minor cuts

Tell a coach about an injury, illness, emotional concern, or family issue that may affect your child during class.

Keep your child home when they have a contagious illness or uncovered skin condition.

Attendance and rescheduling

Core Culture is organized around three planned weekly home-class reservations. These are the student's default classes: a predictable group and rhythm that Sandy can coach over time.

Consistent attendance helps us:

  • Build dependable partner groups
  • Plan the right level of resistance
  • Track your child’s progress
  • Make returning feel more familiar
  • Keep your child connected to the group

When life interrupts a home class, text us. We can reschedule when another class has space and a suitable partner. This protects continuity; it does not guarantee a replacement seat.

When your child resists going to class

It is normal for a child to sometimes feel tired, frustrated, nervous, or embarrassed before class. Ask what is going on first and listen before offering a solution.

If there is pain, illness, fear, a safety concern, or persistent distress, contact Sandy before class and adjust the plan.

If the issue is ordinary reluctance, keeping the normal routine when practical can help. Sandy can lower the starting demand once your child arrives:

  1. Tell the coach what your child said.
  2. Keep the normal plan when the concern is ordinary reluctance.
  3. Let the coach choose an appropriate starting activity.
  4. Speak with the coach when the resistance continues across several classes.

Avoid turning every difficult afternoon into a fresh decision about whether Jiu-Jitsu exists that day. Keep the normal plan when the concern is ordinary reluctance, and tell Sandy what you are seeing.

One hard day may be ordinary friction. If reluctance keeps returning, do not simply push harder. Sandy and the family should identify what is making the experience difficult and adjust the plan.

Competition

Your child may compete, but they do not need to. Students can improve, earn promotions, and belong to the academy without entering a tournament.

For a student who wants the additional test, competition changes the partner, rules, pace, stakes, and amount of coach control. Sandy looks at what remains reliable, what changed, and what should return to class next.

For competitors, we focus on:

  • Student willingness
  • Safe decisions and stoppage awareness
  • Functioning with unfamiliar partners and external rules
  • Recovery after a difficult round
  • Respect
  • Learning from each match and returning with one useful success and one next problem

Winning can matter to a competitor, but one result does not measure your child’s skill or character. Parents handle logistics and support; coaches handle technical direction. Please ask what felt different and what your child would like another chance to try instead of calling out corrections.

When to contact Sandy

Contact us when:

  • Your child reports pain or an injury
  • Your child fears a partner or activity
  • Their behavior changes
  • They feel excluded or unsafe
  • Attendance problems continue
  • They want to quit
  • School or family changes affect training
  • You need help understanding their progress

Share concerns before they grow. Specific information helps us coach your child.
